      How to read the forecast

      Scores for each night

      Deep-sky imaging looks at the hours of astronomical darkness and weighs clear sky most, then moonlight, transparency, seeing and wind.

      Planets & Moon uses every hour after civil dusk and cares mostly about steady air. Moonlight does not matter.

      Stargazing covers naked eye and binoculars from nautical dusk: clear sky, a dark moon and clean air.

      Each score blends the night average with its best three-hour stretch, so a good window still counts on a mixed night.

      Seeing and transparency

      Seeing is how steady the air is, given as the size of a star's blurred disc in arcseconds (smaller is better). It is calculated from the forecast temperature and wind at 16 heights between about 100 m and 16 km, with the Tatarskii and Dewan turbulence formulas, for 500 nm light at the zenith; lower in the sky it is worse. It cannot know about warm roofs, your telescope tube or the ground right next to you, so treat it as a rough guide. From Haarlem, under 1.7″ is a good night and over 2.3″ a poor one.

      Transparency is how clean the air is. It comes from the CAMS aerosol optical depth forecast (about five days ahead), lowered for high humidity and thin high cloud. After that it is estimated from visibility.

      Dew margin is the gap between air temperature and dew point. Below 2 °C your optics will fog without a dew heater.

      Light pollution around Haarlem

      Central Haarlem is roughly Bortle 7 to 8, so the Milky Way is washed out and faint galaxies need narrowband or long integrations. The dunes of Nationaal Park Zuid-Kennemerland, west of the city, are noticeably darker towards the sea.

      The darkest Dutch skies are at the Lauwersmeer and Boschplaat (Terschelling) Dark Sky Parks. Check lightpollutionmap.info for a spot near you.

      The Milky Way's core never rises more than about 9° above the horizon from Haarlem, so summer core shots need a clear, dark southern horizon.
